# Graduated comprehension through a book

Approved as the basis for implementation on November 23, 2026.

## Agreed reading experience

Today, a reader can choose a mix of open-ended and selected-response questions and steer what the model asks. The reader encounters a section’s questions together at its checkpoint. Readers who prefer longer stretches of uninterrupted reading can keep this experience.

The graduated pathway adds optional ephemeral questions during the section. Their primary purpose is to sustain the reader’s attention and help them recover the thread while reading. Question form describes how the reader answers. A question’s role in the pathway determines whether it is ephemeral or intended for Practice.

When preparing a book with ephemeral questions, the reader chooses what should guide their creation and placement.

- **I want ephemeral questions to help me sustain my attention generally.** Place a brief check after a meaningful passage when recalling it will help the reader follow what comes next. A detail can qualify on that basis alone. It may also support a later practice question.
- **I want ephemeral questions to build toward practice questions explicitly.** For each ephemeral question, identify one or more later checkpoint practice questions and the detail, idea, or relationship the reader will use to answer them. Place the check once that material has appeared.

In the explicit preparation option, retain the link from each ephemeral question to each practice question it supports. By default the ephemeral check shows only its own prompt. At the later checkpoint, **Help me recall** can use a linked check to cue the reader before they return to the practice question.

The reader can adjust the frequency of ephemeral questions while reading. For a silent recall check, the ordinary interaction is to recall the answer, reveal the reference, then continue. Continuing completes the check without a correctness grade. If readers lose the thread, they can revisit the relevant passage immediately, then retry with the text hidden or continue reading.

Once the book has introduced enough material for a higher-order practice question, the section checkpoint opens with that question. A reader who wants help can request a short recall prompt and then return to the practice question. It is available even if the reader missed or skipped an earlier check. This same question follows the existing checkpoint and Practice flow, including the reader’s ability to remove a card that has outlived its usefulness.

Ephemeral questions serve the reading session. The question data can persist. The reader can promote a question to Practice as written, edit it manually, or steer a model rewrite around what they want to remember.

A short intermediate check can ask the reader to notice a relationship when doing so helps prepare them for deeper reasoning. Some passages can go directly from recall to a higher-order check.

## Choosing a higher-order question

A higher-order check asks the reader to use what they remember from the book to work something out. Choose the kind of reasoning the passage rewards. Possibilities include explaining a mechanism, inferring or interpreting, comparing and distinguishing, synthesizing across passages, applying an idea to a new case, predicting an outcome, generating an example, and evaluating a claim. Several can overlap. A question may draw on multiple chapters once the reader has encountered the supporting passages.

Judge the thinking required in light of what the book says explicitly. If the author lists three causes of an event, asking for those causes is recall. If the reader has to connect clues across three episodes to explain the event, even a one-sentence answer can demonstrate synthesis.

When an earlier check explicitly supports a practice question, help the reader retrieve the material for that reasoning while leaving the conclusion for them to discover. In a novel, an early check might ask who spoke on a daughter's behalf. After another scene in which she stays silent, the deeper question can ask how the meaning of her silence has changed and which details support that reading.

When several answers are defensible, feedback identifies the evidence a well-supported answer should account for and helps the reader assess how their reasoning connects that evidence to their answer.

## Acceptance examples

Consider a chapter in which a messenger takes a letter to the council. Early on, the reader learns the route and who knows the letter was forged. Later, two councillors interpret its delivery differently. The checkpoint asks the reader to explain their different reactions.

| Reading choice or action | Expected experience |
| --- | --- |
| Prepare the chapter for general attention | A check about the route is eligible if recalling it helps the reader follow the messenger through the next scene. It needs no link to the checkpoint question. |
| Prepare the chapter with explicit links | A check about who knows of the forgery can link to the question about the councillors’ reactions. The check appears after the reader learns who knows, with the checkpoint prompt hidden by default. |
| Skip that check and reach the checkpoint | The reader sees the question about the councillors’ reactions first. Requesting help can bring back the linked check, then return the reader to the same practice question. |
| Complete the chapter and open Practice | The checkpoint question is available under the ordinary Practice rules. The earlier check enters Practice only if the reader promotes it. |
